The Library / When it goes wrong

Big holes, dense everywhere else

A few huge caverns in an otherwise tight, gummy crumb is under-fermentation, however open it looks at first glance.

Why

When a dough hasn't fermented enough, there are too few gas bubbles spread through it. In the oven, what gas there is rushes into a handful of weak spots and blows them up into tunnels, while the dough around them stays dense. Bakers call it fool's crumb, because it looks like the open crumb it isn't.

Further down

A different fault looks similar: one long gap just under the top crust. That is usually an overproofed loaf whose top set while the inside sank away from it, or a pocket of air or flour folded in at shaping. Under-fermented tunnels are scattered through the loaf; the flying crust is a single gap at the top.

Try it

If you see tunnels, give the next bulk 30 to 45 minutes longer, or use the starter nearer its peak.

More from this shelf

  • Underproofed and overproofed look different in the ovenOne bursts open at a random seam. The other spreads flat and barely springs. The bake tells you which mistake you made.
  • Gummy crumb has three quite different causesCut too early, underbaked, or high enzyme activity. They look similar and want opposite fixes.
  • The loaf that came out flatA loaf that spreads into a disc has usually been fermented too far, shaped too loosely, or made too wet for its flour.
  • A crust that stays palePale crust is not enough heat, not enough time uncovered, or a dough that had no sugar left to brown with.
  • A burnt bottomThe base of a pot or stone is much hotter than the air around the loaf. Put something between the two.
  • Stuck to the basketRice flour, more of it than seems necessary, and a dough that went in with a dry, floured surface.
