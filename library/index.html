Myths, tested

Things everyone says. What holds up, and what doesn't.

  • “Dissolve the starter in the water first”You can, and it does no harm. But you don't have to, and whisking it until frothy adds nothing.
  • Autolyse, fermentolyse, or everything at onceThree ways to start a dough, argued about far more than the difference deserves. All three make good bread.
  • “Metal kills your starter”A stainless steel spoon, bowl or whisk does nothing to a starter. Stir away.
  • “A hundred-year-old starter makes better bread”The story is lovely. The microbes don't know about it.
  • “You need high hydration for an open crumb”Water helps, but fermentation and handling decide most of it. A 70% dough can be beautifully open; an 85% one can be dense.
  • “Tap water will kill your starter”For an established starter, tap water is almost always fine.
  • “It has to double before you can use it”Doubling is a useful sign for a liquid wheat starter. For others it's the wrong test.
  • “Throw ice cubes in the pot for steam”In a closed pot, the dough makes all the steam it needs. The ice is optional at best.
  • “Sourdough is fine if you can't eat gluten”It is not gluten-free, and it is not safe for coeliac disease. Some people do find it easier to digest, for a different reason.

When it goes wrong

The usual disasters, and what caused them.

  • The loaf that came out flatA loaf that spreads into a disc has usually been fermented too far, shaped too loosely, or made too wet for its flour.
  • Big holes, dense everywhere elseA few huge caverns in an otherwise tight, gummy crumb is under-fermentation, however open it looks at first glance.
  • A crust that stays palePale crust is not enough heat, not enough time uncovered, or a dough that had no sugar left to brown with.
  • A burnt bottomThe base of a pot or stone is much hotter than the air around the loaf. Put something between the two.
  • Stuck to the basketRice flour, more of it than seems necessary, and a dough that went in with a dry, floured surface.
