How much starter goes in sourdough dough?

The usual amount is 20% of the flour weight: 100 g of starter for 500 g of flour. At 22°C that gives a bulk fermentation of about four hours. Use less starter and the bulk takes longer, use more and it goes faster. The amount is a way to set the clock, and the bread comes out fine across a wide range.

The share of starter measured against the flour is called inoculation.

How the amount changes the bulk

For 500 g of flour at 22°C, with a dough that takes four hours at 20%:

StarterShare of flourBulk takes about
25 g5%10 h 35
50 g10%6 h 30
75 g15%4 h 55
100 g20%4 h
125 g25%3 h 25
150 g30%3 h

These are Sourdough Diary's estimates. Your flour, your starter and your kitchen will move them, so treat the dough as the final word: grown by about half, airy, wobbling when you shake the bowl.

It is not one to one

Half the starter does not double the time. Going from 20% to 10% adds about two and a half hours to a four hour bulk. The culture multiplies as it goes, so a smaller start catches up.

That also means the top of the table gains little. Going from 20% to 30% saves an hour, and brings more acid into the dough from the first minute.

Use it to fit your day

  • Overnight on the counter: 5% starter gives a bulk of around ten hours at 22°C. Mix at ten in the evening, shape at eight.
  • A long workday: 10% in the morning before you leave, in a cool room, and shape in the evening.
  • Bread today: 25 to 30% and a warm spot. See same-day sourdough.

Temperature works on the same clock, and it is the stronger lever: ten degrees roughly doubles the speed. A 10% dough in a 26°C kitchen is about as fast as a 20% dough at 22°C.

Does less starter change the bread?

A long, slow bulk with little starter gives more time for flavour to develop, and usually a more sour loaf. A fast bulk with a lot of starter is milder if the starter was used young.

The dough does not get weaker from less starter. It gets weaker from being left too long, at any amount.

Salt, water and the starter itself

A starter fed with equal parts flour and water is half flour and half water. 100 g of it brings 50 g of each into the dough. If you change the amount of starter by a lot, the dough's hydration moves with it.
